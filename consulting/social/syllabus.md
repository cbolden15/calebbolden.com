# 18-week social content syllabus

Source: `components/tools/ReadinessScorecard.tsx`, the 18-question AI-readiness self-assessment at `/tools/ai-readiness`. Each week below maps to exactly one question from that assessment, in the order it appears there. The 18 weeks are grouped into five monthly batches by dimension, so each month's questions can be filmed or written in a single sitting.

Two pieces per week, built from one question:

- A vertical clip, 45 to 75 seconds, for small-business owners: plumbers, electricians, salon owners, landscapers, and similar trades.
- A LinkedIn essay, 200 to 300 words, for operations leaders at larger SMBs, roughly 20 to 200 people.

Every clip and every essay closes by pointing back to the same instrument: take the assessment at `/tools/ai-readiness`.

---

## Month 1: data and systems (Weeks 1-4)

### Week 1: Is your customer and job information in one place, rather than scattered across email, texts, spreadsheets, and paper?

**Dimension:** data and systems

**Clip:** 45 to 75 seconds, owner-facing. An electrician on camera, checking his phone for a customer's texts, then a paper work order clipped to the dash, then a spreadsheet file named something like "customers_FINAL_v3.xlsx" that hasn't been opened in months. A repeat customer calls about a panel upgrade quote from three months back; he checks all three, calls back with the wrong number pulled from the wrong source, and has to call a second time. Close on what a single job history would have made that call: one minute, not two calls. End on the assessment CTA.

**Essay:** 200 to 300 words, operator-facing. An ops lead at a 55-person electrical and HVAC contractor running four crews. Customer and job data lives across a scheduling app, a shared billing spreadsheet, and one dispatcher's memory of which crew prefers which customers. When a customer disputes a service date, resolving it takes three people cross-checking sources, usually 20 to 30 minutes. At roughly 15 disputes a month, that is over 6 hours a week of an ops coordinator's time spent reconciling instead of scheduling. Name it as a "where does the truth live" problem rather than a software problem, and point to the assessment's first section as the way to measure it before buying anything.

### Week 2: Could you pull a clean list of your last 12 months of customers or jobs in under 10 minutes?

**Dimension:** data and systems

**Clip:** 45 to 75 seconds, owner-facing. A landscaper is asked, on camera, to pull last year's customer list right now. He opens QuickBooks, exports to a spreadsheet, and realizes half of last spring's jobs were invoiced under a different business name after a rebrand. Twenty minutes in, he still doesn't have a clean list. Close on what he could do with that list today, a mailer to last year's mulch customers before the reorder window closes, and why he isn't doing it. End on the assessment CTA.

**Essay:** 200 to 300 words, operator-facing. An ops director at a 90-person facilities maintenance company running 8 regional teams, each on its own job tracker. A renewal campaign needs a list of every customer serviced in the last year; pulling it takes an analyst four days of merging exports and de-duplicating account names by hand. The campaign missed its launch window by three weeks, and at their average contract size that delay sat on six figures of revenue. "Under 10 minutes" is a test of whether a growth initiative can move at the speed the market allows, not a convenience metric. Run the assessment before the next campaign misses its window the same way.

### Week 3: Do you avoid typing the same customer information into more than one system?

**Dimension:** data and systems

**Clip:** 45 to 75 seconds, owner-facing. A salon owner takes a new client's info at the front desk into the booking app, then retypes the same name, phone, and email onto a paper intake card for the stylist, then again into a loyalty spreadsheet. Three entries, one client. Close on what happens the third time a digit gets mistyped: the client stops getting appointment reminders and no-shows, assuming she confirmed. End on the assessment CTA.

**Essay:** 200 to 300 words, operator-facing. An operations manager at a 40-person med-spa group with 3 locations. Client demographic and consent data gets entered separately into a scheduling platform, a billing system, and a compliance log at intake, because none of the three talk to each other. At roughly 90 seconds of re-keying per client and 3,000 visits a month, that is close to 75 hours of paid staff time a month spent moving the same six fields between systems that should hand off automatically. Call it the quiet leak: unglamorous, but it compounds with volume, which is exactly why the assessment scores it before any of the three systems get replaced.

### Week 4: Could a new hire find a past customer's history without asking a coworker?

**Dimension:** data and systems

**Clip:** 45 to 75 seconds, owner-facing. A plumber's new hire, first week, gets a callback: the customer says "you were just here in June for the same leak." He has no way to check without radioing the owner, who is under a different sink and can't get to his phone for twenty minutes. Close on the job that should have taken 15 minutes, because it was a callback with a diagnosis already on file, turning into an hour of unpaid re-diagnosis. End on the assessment CTA.

**Essay:** 200 to 300 words, operator-facing. An ops lead at a 70-person residential services company with heavy seasonal hiring. Every summer they add a dozen new techs, and every summer those techs re-diagnose problems a previous tech already solved, because job history is scattered across old work orders scanned as PDFs with no search. An internal estimate puts the cost at 2 extra hours per repeat job across roughly 150 repeat jobs a season, which is 300 hours of billable-adjacent time lost to onboarding friction alone. What this question actually tests is whether the system holds knowledge independent of who's been there longest, not how senior anyone is. Worth running the assessment before the next hiring wave adds more of that same fragility.

## Month 2: process documentation (Weeks 5-8)

### Week 5: If you were out sick for a week, could someone run your busiest process from written instructions alone?

**Dimension:** process documentation

**Clip:** 45 to 75 seconds, owner-facing. A landscaping crew stands around the truck at 7am with no route sheet, because the owner is the one who mentally sequences the day's stops by traffic and mower type, and today he's out sick. Without him they guess, backtrack across town twice, and finish two jobs short by 5pm. Close on the fact that a written sequencing rule would have cost him one afternoon to write, once. End on the assessment CTA.

**Essay:** 200 to 300 words, operator-facing. An operations manager at the regional office of a 30-location cleaning franchise group (110 total staff). The busiest process, onboarding a new commercial account, exists only in two regional trainers' heads. When one trainer took a two-week leave, three new accounts sat un-onboarded, and one canceled before it ever billed an invoice. Named in dollars, that single lost account was worth more than a year of the pay gap between a trainer and a documented process. The deliberate framing here is "busiest," not "most complex": this section is testing the process that would hurt most if it stalled, which is rarely the one people think to write down first. Find that process with the assessment before it becomes the one that stalls.

### Week 6: Do your 3 most common tasks have a written step-by-step, rather than living in someone's head?

**Dimension:** process documentation

**Clip:** 45 to 75 seconds, owner-facing. Three different electricians, asked on camera to describe how they close out a job (what photos, what paperwork, what customer sign-off), each give a different answer, side by side. Close on the reframe: there's no single correct answer yet to train anyone to, which makes this a standards gap, not a training gap. End on the assessment CTA.

**Essay:** 200 to 300 words, operator-facing. An ops lead at a 50-person HVAC and electrical company. The three most common tasks, service call intake, close-out, and warranty claims, were never written down because the two founders "just know" them and trained everyone by shadowing. Regional hires each picked up a slightly different close-out, and warranty claims now get rejected at nearly double the rate at one branch compared to another, for the same manufacturer. Quantify the gap across a quarter's rejected claims. Undocumented process doesn't fail evenly: it fails hardest wherever the newest hire happened to learn from whoever cut a corner. The assessment's process questions are built to find that corner before a customer does.

### Week 7: When a mistake happens, can you point to the exact step where it went wrong?

**Dimension:** process documentation

**Clip:** 45 to 75 seconds, owner-facing. A salon client's color comes out wrong. The owner and the stylist go back and forth: was it the formula, the timing, or a note the previous colorist never wrote down? Nobody can say, because there's no record of which step was actually followed. Close on the point that without a written sequence, every mistake becomes a debate instead of a fix. End on the assessment CTA.

**Essay:** 200 to 300 words, operator-facing. An operations director at a 65-person multi-location auto service group. A batch of oil changes used the wrong filter part number for three days before a customer complaint caught it. The post-mortem takes two weeks, because nobody can reconstruct which link in the ordering-to-install chain broke: mislabeled inventory, a skipped part-number check, or an unflagged supplier substitution. Name the rework and recall cost across the affected vehicles. Process debt shows up first as a slow post-mortem, well before it shows up as a major incident, and a slow post-mortem is the leading indicator worth catching. Catch it with the assessment before the incident does the catching instead.

### Week 8: Does your team do the same task the same way regardless of who is doing it?

**Dimension:** process documentation

**Clip:** 45 to 75 seconds, owner-facing. Split screen: two crew leads mowing lawns of the same size, one finishing in 25 minutes with a clear pattern, the other in 40 minutes with backtracking. Neither is doing it wrong exactly, but the business is paying for the gap every week. Close on what standardizing the pattern recovers: 15 minutes, times every lawn, times every week. End on the assessment CTA.

**Essay:** 200 to 300 words, operator-facing. An ops manager at a 45-person commercial landscaping company running 6 crews. Crew leads each developed their own site-walk sequence over the years, so estimate-to-actual time varies by up to 30% on jobs that should be near-identical. That variance makes bidding unreliable: two crews can bid what looks like the same job and be off by hundreds of dollars depending on which one wins it. Name the estimating-error range across a quarter's bids. The real value of this question is measuring whether the business can predict its own costs, not scoring consistency for its own sake. That gap is exactly what the assessment is built to price out before the next round of bids goes out uneven again.

## Month 3: repetition and volume (Weeks 9-11)

### Week 9: Is there a task you or your team does more than 5 times a week that feels repetitive?

**Dimension:** repetition and volume

**Clip:** 45 to 75 seconds, owner-facing. A plumber texts back the same "what's your address and what's the issue" questions to every new lead, by hand, 8 to 10 times a week, nearly word for word. Say it plainly on camera: "I've probably typed this exact paragraph 400 times this year." Close on the reframe that repetition, not complexity, is the signal this section is looking for. End on the assessment CTA.

**Essay:** 200 to 300 words, operator-facing. An ops lead at an 80-person property management company. The leasing team manually copies the same welcome-packet email to every new tenant, roughly 15 times a week across properties. It isn't hard, which is why nobody flagged it, but at 6 minutes each, that's 6 hours of staff time a week spent on something with zero judgment involved. The assessment scores a "yes" here as a positive signal on purpose: repetition at volume is exactly the shape of problem worth solving first, not a mark against the business. Take it to find where else that same shape is hiding.

### Week 10: Does someone spend more than 2 hours a week on data entry or copy-pasting between systems?

**Dimension:** repetition and volume

**Clip:** 45 to 75 seconds, owner-facing. A salon's front desk person re-keys the day's appointments from the booking app into a separate spreadsheet for commission tracking, every day, about 20 minutes a day. Close on why this survives for years unaddressed: it's never dramatic enough to notice on its own. End on the assessment CTA.

**Essay:** 200 to 300 words, operator-facing. An operations manager at a 120-person multi-branch dental group. A billing coordinator manually re-enters insurance claim data from the practice management system into the clearinghouse portal because the two don't sync, about 3 hours a week per branch across 8 branches. That's 24 hours a week, over half a full-time role, spent moving data that already exists somewhere else in the business. This question isolates one specific pattern, copy-paste between two systems that already both hold the data, because it's close to the cheapest kind of automation to justify. The assessment is where to find every instance of it before the next hire inherits the habit.

### Week 11: Do you answer certain customer questions with nearly identical wording every time?

**Dimension:** repetition and volume

**Clip:** 45 to 75 seconds, owner-facing. Three different text threads with three different customers, all asking some version of "when's my next mow," and the landscaper typing nearly the same reply each time. Close on the point that if the answer is almost always the same words, the customer didn't need to wait for him personally to type it. End on the assessment CTA.

**Essay:** 200 to 300 words, operator-facing. An ops director at a 35-person managed IT services provider. Support tickets asking things like "how do I reset my VPN" get answered by whichever technician picks them up, each writing the answer from scratch in their own words, none of it saved anywhere reusable. Across 200 tickets a month, an estimated 40% are a repeat of a question answered within the last 90 days. Near-identical answers are the cheapest kind of automation to justify, because the content already exists; it just isn't captured anywhere. The assessment's repetition section is built to find exactly that gap.

## Month 4: team and buy-in (Weeks 12-14)

### Week 12: Would your staff try a new tool if it saved them time, rather than resisting any change?

**Dimension:** team and buy-in

**Clip:** 45 to 75 seconds, owner-facing. An electrician describes trying to roll out a new scheduling app last year: half the crew adopted it within a week, the other half kept texting him directly out of habit, so he ended up running two systems in parallel, which was worse than before. Close on the point that the rollout failed before the tool ever had a chance to, which makes this a team question, not a technology one. End on the assessment CTA.

**Essay:** 200 to 300 words, operator-facing. An operations manager at a 95-person warehousing and logistics company. A prior rollout of a digital inventory scanner failed within two months because three senior floor leads never adopted it, and their teams followed their lead rather than the mandate from ops. The tool tested well; the resistance was social, not technical. The scanner subscription was paid for 9 months with near-zero usage before it was cancelled. This question is built to catch that failure mode before money is spent on it again, because team readiness predicts adoption better than a feature list does. Run the assessment before the next rollout repeats it.

### Week 13: Is there at least one person besides you who would champion a new tool internally?

**Dimension:** team and buy-in

**Clip:** 45 to 75 seconds, owner-facing. A salon owner describes being the only one who cares whether a new tool gets used: she sets it up, she's the only one who logs in, and within a month everyone is back to the old way because there was no second person keeping it alive once she got busy with clients again. Close on the point that one champion who isn't the owner is often the difference between adoption and a subscription nobody uses. End on the assessment CTA.

**Essay:** 200 to 300 words, operator-facing. An ops lead at a 55-person multi-site veterinary group. A prior rollout of a client-communication tool succeeded at 2 of 5 locations and failed at the other 3. In hindsight, the successful locations each had an office manager personally pushing daily use, while the other 3 relied on the same central mandate with no local champion. This question is about organizational structure, not enthusiasm: a champion needs standing with the team on the ground, not just goodwill toward the idea. The assessment is built to ask who that person actually is before the next tool gets bought.

### Week 14: When your team suggests a shortcut or tool, does it get a real follow-up?

**Dimension:** team and buy-in

**Clip:** 45 to 75 seconds, owner-facing. A landscaping crew member mentions, almost in passing, an app he uses on the side that would cut route planning time in half. The owner says "huh, interesting," and it never comes up again for three months. Close on the point that good ideas are dying from silence, not disagreement: a bottleneck, not a rejection. End on the assessment CTA.

**Essay:** 200 to 300 words, operator-facing. An operations director at a 140-person regional retail chain. Store associates raise process suggestions in monthly meetings; a review of the last year's meeting notes found 40 suggestions logged and 3 with any documented follow-up. Whether any of the other 37 were good ideas is unknowable, because none were tested. At minimum, the meeting time spent gathering suggestions that go nowhere is a standing cost with zero return. Readiness here is a workflow question, whether an idea has a defined next step, more than it is a culture question. The assessment's team section is where to check whether that step exists.

## Month 5: budget and focus (Weeks 15-18)

### Week 15: Could you set aside $100 to $300 a month to test a tool that saved 5+ hours a week?

**Dimension:** budget and focus

**Clip:** 45 to 75 seconds, owner-facing. A plumber does quick math on camera: 5 hours a week at what his time or his dispatcher's time is worth, against a $200-a-month tool. The math clearly favors the tool, but he admits he's never actually run these numbers before, he just assumed software like this was for bigger companies. Close on the point that the real barrier is usually the missing arithmetic, not the $200. End on the assessment CTA.

**Essay:** 200 to 300 words, operator-facing. An ops lead at a 25-person specialty contracting firm. A proposed scheduling automation would cost $250 a month and save an estimated 6 hours a week of manual dispatch adjustments. Leadership sat on it for two quarters, not because the return was unclear on paper, but because nobody had priced what those 6 hours currently cost in dispatcher time and missed-job opportunity. Once that number was written down, roughly $1,800 a month in loaded labor cost, approval took a week. The budget question is really about whether the cost of the status quo has ever been priced at all, not about affordability. Price it with the assessment before sitting on another proposal for two quarters.

### Week 16: Can you name ONE specific task you want help with, rather than "we should use AI"?

**Dimension:** budget and focus

**Clip:** 45 to 75 seconds, owner-facing. An electrician admits that when he first got curious about "AI for the business," he had no concrete task in mind, just a vague sense he should be doing something. Contrast that with naming one precise task on camera: auto-drafting the follow-up text after every estimate. Close on the point that specificity is what turns curiosity into something that can actually be built or bought. End on the assessment CTA.

**Essay:** 200 to 300 words, operator-facing. An operations manager at a 200-person manufacturing SMB. Leadership approved an "AI initiative" with no named task, and six months later, a hired consultant and a licensed platform had produced no deployed use case, because nobody had defined what problem it was solving. The fix was starting over with one narrow, named task, auto-summarizing daily production logs for the morning stand-up, which shipped in three weeks. Vague enthusiasm and a real project are different things, and this question is a forcing function to tell them apart before any money moves. The assessment is that forcing function, worth running before the next initiative gets approved without a task attached.

### Week 17: If you have abandoned a tool before, do you know why it did not stick?

**Dimension:** budget and focus

**Clip:** 45 to 75 seconds, owner-facing. A salon owner names a booking app she paid for and dropped within two months, and admits she still isn't sure why: maybe training, maybe it just didn't fit how her stylists actually worked. Close on the point that not knowing why the last tool failed means the next one is likely to fail for the same unexamined reason. End on the assessment CTA.

**Essay:** 200 to 300 words, operator-facing. An ops director at a 60-person home services company. Two prior software rollouts, a CRM and then a route-optimization tool, both quietly died within a quarter, and until this question forced the exercise, nobody had run a real post-mortem on either one. Doing it surfaced the same root cause both times: the tool was chosen and configured by ops leadership without input from the technicians who'd use it daily, so field adoption never happened. A documented failure pattern is worth more going into the next purchase than any vendor's feature comparison, because it's specific to this team. The assessment is where to document it, before the next rollout repeats the same root cause.

### Week 18: Do you know roughly what an hour of your own time is worth to the business?

**Dimension:** budget and focus

**Clip:** 45 to 75 seconds, owner-facing. A landscaper is asked point blank what his hour is worth. He guesses low, then does rough math against revenue and realizes it's meaningfully higher, which reframes every hour he spends on invoicing instead of bidding new work. Close on the point that this number is the one that should decide what gets delegated or automated first. End on the assessment CTA.

**Essay:** 200 to 300 words, operator-facing. An operations manager at a 40-person specialty trade contractor. Leadership had never priced the owner's or the ops director's time, so hour-for-hour decisions, like whether the director should personally review every timesheet, were made on habit rather than math. Once the director's loaded hourly value was estimated against typical market rates for the role, it became clear that 4 hours a week of manual timesheet review cost more than the software that would remove it. This is the last question in the assessment for a reason: it's the number every other answer eventually gets measured against. Take the assessment and find out what that number actually is.
