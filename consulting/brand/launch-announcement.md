Most of the AI failures I watch up close have nothing to do with the AI.

A shop buys a tool to fix scheduling. Six weeks later it is switched off. The tool worked fine. The process it plugged into was never written down, so nobody could say what "working" was supposed to look like, and the first week it produced an answer somebody disagreed with, it lost. Gartner predicts that 30% of generative AI projects will be abandoned after proof of concept. At the small-business end of the market that number feels low, because there is no team to absorb a failed rollout and no budget to try again in the same year.

I am starting an AI consulting practice for local small businesses, and that pattern is the whole reason it exists.

Owners and operators are being sold automation for processes nobody has mapped. Automate a process you have not fixed and you have not removed the waste. You have made it faster and put it on a subscription. So I do the boring part first: map the process, find the bottleneck, cut the steps that should not exist, then automate what is left. That is lean discipline pointed at AI instead of at a factory floor. [placeholder: one line on where Caleb learned lean and six sigma, and roughly when]

I am not describing this from the sidelines. I run Vora, a production AI CRM serving 11 service verticals with real billing attached, and I build systems like it every day. That is also how I know how many of them should never have been built.

If you want to know where your business actually stands, the first diagnostic is free: calebbolden.com/tools/ai-readiness. Eighteen questions, scored across five dimensions.
