# Elevator pitches

Spoken scripts. Written to be said out loud, not read off a page. Facts here come from
consulting/brand/brand-context.md and consulting/launch-roadmap.md. Anything in brackets is a
gap to fill with a real detail, not a suggestion to improvise.

## 1. Ten-second intro (26 words)

I'm Caleb Bolden. I help local businesses fix a broken process first, then automate it. Most
AI projects skip that step, and that's why they fail.

Delivery: even pace, no rush. Small pause after "first." Land on "fail" and stop talking.

## 2. Thirty-second networking pitch (85 words)

I'm Caleb Bolden. I run an AI product company, and I do AI consulting for local businesses.
Here's the thing I keep seeing. An owner buys a tool, it doesn't stick, and they blame the AI.
Usually the process underneath it was already broken. So I map the workflow first, the way
you'd do a lean value stream map, fix what's wasteful, and only then automate what's left.
You probably see the same messes I do. Who's the most frustrated client on your books?

Delivery: conversational, slightly faster than the intro. Real pause after "already broken,"
that's the line they'll remember. Ask the last question and let it sit. At a chamber table
where nobody has clients to refer, swap the closing question for "What's the thing your team
redoes every week?" If they want something to take away, point them at the readiness
assessment on the site.

## 3. Sixty-second founder story (~150 words with the placeholder filled)

I build AI software for a living. My company, Vora Technologies, runs a production CRM used
across eleven service verticals, with real billing and real customers depending on it. So I'm
not guessing about what this technology can do.

What surprised me was how often it doesn't work for small businesses. Gartner predicted 30
percent of these projects get abandoned right after the proof of concept. BCG found 74
percent of companies can't point to any tangible value. That's not a model problem. The
software works fine. It got dropped on top of a process that was already broken.

one process that was broken, plus the detail that made it click for you. Two sentences, said
plainly.]

So I do it in that order now. Map the process. Fix it. Then automate what's left.

Delivery: slow down for the two stats and name the firms out loud, they do the credibility
work. Drop your voice for the placeholder story, that's the human part. The last three
sentences are three separate beats, pause between each one.

## 4. Tagline

I fix the process first, then I automate it.

Delivery: say it as a full stop, not a slogan. Works as a sign-off in email and as an answer
to "so what do you do?"

## 5. Value proposition

For local businesses buried in repetitive admin, I map how the work actually runs, cut the
waste, and automate what's left, so the technology lands on a process that works.

Delivery: one breath, steady. Emphasize "actually runs." This is the sentence for a proposal
cover or an intro email, not for a noisy room.

## 6. Closing line

Automate a broken process and all you've got is a faster mess.

Delivery: say it, pause, then stop. Do not add a sentence after it. Works to close a
lunch-and-learn or to end a first meeting.
