### Recurring patterns (the actual bar, as observed)

1. **Headshots are inconsistent even among established solo practitioners.** Only 2 of 6 (Ray Sidney-Smith, Brennan Dunn) showed a confirmed personal photo on the fetched page; the rest were `unverified`, not confirmed absent. A real photo is a differentiator when present, but its absence on a single page does not disqualify a practitioner from this set — several of these people are trusted operators without one visible on the fetched surface.
2. **Numbers are rare in on-page case studies, common in third-party citation.** None of the six published a named-client, quantified case study on the fetched page. The pattern that recurs instead: qualitative scenario write-ups (Nora Schlesinger), personal-story-as-proof (Brennan Dunn, Justin Welsh), or testimonial quotes without company names (Rachel Woods). Where hard numbers appear at all, they arrive through a third party — a podcast title, a guest's own words — not a self-published metrics chart.
3. **Certifications are not load-bearing for this bar**, including for the one lean/six-sigma practitioner in the set. Jamie Flinchbaugh's hero content leads with leadership positioning language, not a Black Belt badge. This tempers any recommendation to prominently display Caleb's lean/six-sigma certification as a primary credibility device; it may matter more as supporting detail than as a hero-level signal.
4. **Pricing visibility splits by proximity to the SMB buyer.** The fractional-COO practitioner selling directly to small businesses (Nora Schlesinger) publishes a starting price. The two productized/solo-authority practitioners selling premium or high-ticket engagements (Brennan Dunn, and by pattern Justin Welsh's cohort pricing) keep price off the homepage and let it surface through sales conversation or third-party mentions instead.
5. **Proof before a track record leans on borrowed authority, not fabricated authority.** Every expert in this set who lacked hard client numbers substituted something real and checkable: a testimonial from a real (if not fully named) person, a named podcast appearance, a press quote from an identifiable author, or a description of the practitioner's own business operations. None simulated a client result. This matches the brand's honesty guardrail rather than contradicting it.

### Differentiation test

Caleb's three candidate differentiators, checked against the researched set rather than asserted.

**The working-wall visual system (blueprint-blue, value-stream-map aesthetic).** Holds. None of the six researched sites use anything resembling a literal process-map or drafting-instrument visual language. Nora Schlesinger and Jamie Flinchbaugh run generic template-driven consulting sites (Squarespace and a WordPress slider theme respectively); Ray Sidney-Smith and Brennan Dunn run plain WordPress brochure/blog sites; Rachel Woods runs a modern SaaS-style Webflow site; Justin Welsh runs a minimalist newsletter site. None depict their own methodology visually the way the working wall does. This differentiator holds cleanly.

**The measure-before-publishing stance.** Holds, but weakly, and the weak form should be stated plainly rather than oversold. None of the six explicitly articulate "we don't publish a claim we can't measure" as a stated position anywhere in the fetched content — it is not a line any of them say. What does hold is the pattern in 3b.5: none of them fabricated a client result to cover the same proof gap Caleb has. So the stance is differentiated in that Caleb states it outright as a named principle, while the researched set merely doesn't violate it. Stage C should frame this as "we found no researched expert who states this as a principle," not "we found experts who violate it."

**The live assistant (a working agent embedded in the site).** Does not hold as verified, and should not be claimed as verified. A text-mode fetch of a single page per expert cannot reliably detect a chat widget, since it may load client-side or sit outside the fetched content region. No live assistant was observed on any of the six fetched pages, but this is an absence-of-evidence result, not evidence of absence — an accurate test would require loading each site in a browser, which was out of scope for this pass. Stage C should mark this differentiator `unverified, not claimed` rather than asserting it holds.
