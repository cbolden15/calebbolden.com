# Kaizen opportunity workshop

Facilitator guide, half day. Converts the annotated map into a ranked shortlist of automation candidates, ready for formal scoring.

## Purpose

Two jobs, in order:

1. Turn the waste already identified on the map into a named list of automation candidates, sized well enough to score.
2. Keep the client honest about what is not an AI problem. Some of what looks like a great automation candidate is actually a broken process that automation would just make faster and more broken.

If this session produces five solid candidates and one clear "this is a process fix, not a tech fix" call, it worked. A pile of vague ideas or a list of tool names is a sign to slow down and redo the sort.

## Attendees

4 to 8 people. The owner or a delegate with budget authority, plus the people who actually run the mapped process day to day. Skip anyone who only manages the process on paper. The person who answers the phone or keys the invoice knows more about where the time goes than the person who signs their paycheck.

If the group is larger than 8, split into two sessions by process area rather than cramming everyone into one room. Cross-talk between 10 people produces noise, not candidates.

## Prep (day before)

- Print or project the annotated value stream map. If it's on paper, tape it to a wall where people can stand next to it and point.
- Circulate the findings memo at least 24 hours ahead. Ask attendees to skim it, not study it. The point is familiarity, not homework.
- Pull a seed list from the map overlay: every step marked as a "strong candidate" (high data availability, low decision complexity, clear handoff) goes on a starter list. This isn't the final list. It's kindling.
- Print or write out the three-way sort buckets somewhere visible: Automate, Redesign, Not now.
- Bring sticky notes or dot stickers for the ranking step. Low tech is fine. A whiteboard and markers work as well as any app.

## Agenda (half day, 4 hours)

| Time | Block | Output |
|---|---|---|
| 0:00 to 0:15 | Open, restate purpose, ground rules | Everyone knows why they're in the room |
| 0:15 to 0:45 | Walk the map, name the pain out loud | Pain points confirmed, nothing new invented yet |
| 0:45 to 1:30 | Three-way sort | Every waste item bucketed: Automate, Redesign, Not now |
| 1:30 to 1:40 | Break | |
| 1:40 to 2:20 | Candidate generation (seeded by use-case categories) | Long list of named candidates |
| 2:20 to 3:05 | Rough sizing per candidate | Hours/week, who does it, what breaks, on a shared sheet |
| 3:05 to 3:35 | Dot vote / forced ranking | Shortlist of 5 to 8 candidates |
| 3:35 to 4:00 | Recap, assign owners, set next step | Shortlist handed off for formal scoring |

Adjust block lengths for group size, not the total. An 8-person group needs the full 45 minutes on the sort; a 4-person group can compress it to 30 and bank the time for sizing.

## Facilitation sequence

### 1. Walk the map's pain points aloud (30 min)

Stand at the map. Go step by step in process order, not by whoever's loudest. For each step, ask the person who runs it: "What's slow, annoying, or error-prone here?" Write their answer on the map or a parking lot sheet next to the step, in their words.

Do not solve anything yet. If someone jumps to "we should just get an AI to do that," say: "Hold that thought, we'll get to solutions in twenty minutes. Right now I just want the pain named." This is the single most common failure of this block: solving before the room has agreed on what's actually broken.

By the end of this block you should have a pain point written next to most steps on the map, in the words of the person who does the work.

### 2. The three-way sort (45 min)

This is the heart of the session. Every pain point named in block one goes into exactly one of three buckets:

- **Fixable by AI or automation.** The bottleneck is volume, speed, or manual data movement, and the process itself is sound.
- **Fixable by process redesign, no technology needed.** The bottleneck is a bad handoff, an unnecessary approval step, unclear ownership, or a rule nobody remembers the reason for.
- **Not worth fixing now.** Low frequency, low cost, or tangled up with a bigger decision the client isn't ready to make.

Say this out loud before starting the sort, close to word for word: "If the process is broken, automating it just makes the wrong thing happen faster. Before anything goes in the Automate bucket, we have to believe the process itself is right, just slow or manual. If the real problem is that three people have to approve a $200 purchase, no chatbot fixes that. We fix the approval rule first."

Go item by item. For each pain point, ask: "Is this a speed and volume problem, or is this a 'the process itself doesn't make sense' problem?" Let the room argue it out, but you make the final call on the bucket if there's a stalemate after two minutes. Don't let one item eat ten minutes.

Watch for items that split. "Invoice approval takes too long" might be half redesign (why does it need three signatures) and half automation candidate (once approved, entry into the accounting system is manual and slow). Split the item into two and sort each half.

The Redesign bucket doesn't get scored in this engagement, but it doesn't get dropped either. Write it into the findings as a process recommendation, separate from the AI backlog. Clients need to see it was heard, not silently discarded because it doesn't fit the AI narrative they hired you for.

### 3. Candidate generation, primed by use-case categories (40 min)

For everything that landed in the Automate bucket, turn it into a named candidate: a specific process step, not a general area. Use these categories as prompts if the room stalls, reading them one at a time and asking "does anything like this happen here":

- Customer comms triage (calls, texts, emails that need sorting or a first response)
- Quoting and estimating
- Scheduling and dispatch
- Data entry between systems (nothing talks to anything else)
- Invoice and AP processing
- Marketing content (social posts, review requests, follow-up emails)
- Reporting (someone manually assembles a number every week or month)
- Document intake (forms, contracts, applications that arrive as paper or PDF and need to become structured data)

For each candidate, write one sentence naming the exact step it fixes. "A chatbot" is not a candidate. "Answering the 30 calls a week that just ask about pricing" is. If someone proposes a candidate as a tool name ("we need a CRM" or "let's get an AI phone system"), ask: "What's the actual task that tool would be doing?" and write down the task, not the tool.

Aim for 10 to 15 named candidates by the end of this block. More than that is fine, fewer than 8 means block one or two didn't surface enough, and it's worth a second pass around the map.

### 4. Rough sizing per candidate (45 min)

For each candidate, capture four numbers or facts on a shared sheet, one row per candidate:

- Hours per week currently spent on this task
- Who does it (role, not name, unless it's a one-person shop)
- What breaks or goes wrong when it's rushed or skipped
- Any known blocker (the data doesn't exist digitally, it lives in someone's head, the current software has no way to export)

Keep this fast. Thirty seconds of debate per number is enough at this stage; the formal scoring pass afterward is where precision matters. If the room can't agree on hours per week within a wide range, write down the range and move on.

### 5. Dot vote or forced ranking (30 min)

Give each attendee 3 to 5 dot votes (physical stickers or tally marks) to place on the candidates they think matter most. Owners' votes don't count extra, but note separately if the owner strongly favors or strongly opposes a candidate the room ranked differently. That signal matters later.

Alternative for smaller groups: forced ranking. Everyone writes their top 5 candidates in order, tally the results, take the top 5 to 8 by combined rank.

Either way, the output is a shortlist of 5 to 8 candidates that moves forward to formal scoring (see the opportunity scoring matrix). Don't score in this room. Dot votes tell you what the room feels; the scoring matrix tells you what's actually worth doing, and the two don't always agree. That gap itself is useful data, so write down any candidate that scored high on votes but looks weak on paper.

## Ground rules

State these at the open, and repeat them if the room drifts.

- No tool names during idea generation. Not "Zapier," not "ChatGPT," not "an AI agent." Name the task, not the technology. Tool selection happens after scoring, not before.
- Every candidate names the specific process step it fixes. If a candidate can't be pointed to on the map, it isn't a candidate yet, it's a feeling.
- "A chatbot" is not a candidate. "Answering the 30 calls a week that just ask about pricing" is.
- Nobody defends their own job in this room. If someone's task is a strong automation candidate, that's a signal to shift them to higher-value work, not a threat. Say this plainly if you sense tension: nobody's losing their job over a shortlist.
- Disagreement goes on the map or the parking lot sheet, not into a side conversation. If two people argue about whether something belongs in Automate or Redesign, write both views down and keep moving.

## Common failure modes and how to steer out of them

**The room jumps straight to tool names.** ("We just need an AI receptionist.") Redirect to the task: "What would that receptionist actually be doing, minute to minute?" Write the task down, drop the tool name.

**Everything gets sorted into Automate.** Usually means the redesign question isn't being asked hard enough. Pick the three biggest items in the Automate bucket and ask directly: "If we changed nothing about the technology, could we fix this by changing who does what, or when?" If yes, move it.

**The owner overrules the room on sizing.** Owners often underestimate hours spent on tasks they don't personally do, or overestimate ones they find personally annoying. When owner and staff estimates diverge by more than 2x, write down both numbers rather than picking one. Let the scoring pass sort it out later with real data if possible.

**Candidates stay vague.** "Improve customer communication" is not sizeable. Push back every time: "Which step, which channel, which volume?" If a candidate can't survive that question, it's not ready, park it and revisit at the end if time allows.

**The session runs long on the sort and starves candidate generation.** Set a visible timer for the sort block. If it's not done at 45 minutes, take the remaining ungrouped items into a five-minute lightning round: quick gut call, revisit later if wrong.

**Someone tries to solve a candidate mid-session.** ("We could build that with...") Acknowledge and park it. "Great, write that on the candidate sheet as a note, we're not designing solutions yet, just naming and sizing them."

**The list is dominated by one department.** If seven of ten candidates come from the same function, ask directly whether other functions were represented in block one. It might be accurate (that function really is the bottleneck), or it might mean quieter attendees didn't get room to speak. Circle back to anyone who hasn't contributed by the midpoint of block one.
