# Objection notes

This is a diagnostic reference, not a script. Don't memorize these and recite them on a call, that reads as rehearsed and erodes trust. Read them beforehand to understand the shape of each objection, then respond in your own words, in the moment, to what the person actually said.

An objection means the prospect doesn't yet believe they need this badly enough, not that they need a better rebuttal. After every call where an objection comes up, log it below (date, prospect, exact wording) and ask what part of the offer, the pricing, or the positioning caused it. Fix the offer element, don't just script a harder answer.

Each objection below follows the same shape: Acknowledge the concern as real, Diagnose it with a question instead of arguing, Reframe using what they tell you, then Bridge to a concrete next step.

## "This is going to replace jobs"

**Acknowledge:** That's a real concern, and a reasonable one to raise before bringing anyone in.

**Diagnose:** "What made you think of that, specifically? Is there a role or a person you're picturing?"

**Reframe:** Most of what gets automated in a small business isn't a job, it's the retyping, the double-checking, and the waiting that eats a job's time. The goal is usually to give the people already there less busywork, not fewer people.

**Bridge:** "Part of the discovery process is mapping exactly where the manual work is, before anything gets touched. Nothing gets automated without you seeing the map first and deciding what's in scope."

## "We tried a chatbot before and it failed"

**Acknowledge:** That's a common outcome, and it's usually not the business's fault.

**Diagnose:** "What did that project actually look like? Who built it, and what was it supposed to do?"

**Reframe:** Most failed chatbot projects started with a tool, not a workflow. Somebody bought software before anyone mapped how the work actually happens, so the tool ended up bolted onto a broken process instead of fixing one.

**Bridge:** "That's exactly why the first phase here is mapping, not building. If we don't find a real automation candidate, I'll tell you that instead of selling you a tool."

## "What happens to our data / is this safe"

**Acknowledge:** That's the right question to ask before letting anyone near your systems.

**Diagnose:** "What specifically are you worried about, customer data, financial records, something else?"

**Reframe:** Credential and data handling follows a written policy, not ad hoc access. See `client-credential-policy.md`: no passwords over email or chat, scoped access only, everything revoked at the end of the engagement.

**Bridge:** "I can walk you through exactly how access works before we sign anything, so there's no surprise once we start."

## "This seems too complicated for a business our size"

**Acknowledge:** Fair. A lot of AI marketing is written for companies much bigger than yours.

**Diagnose:** "What part feels complicated, the technology itself, or figuring out where to even start?"

**Reframe:** The starting point isn't a platform decision, it's a conversation about where your team's time actually goes. That part isn't complicated, it's just a few honest questions about your day.

**Bridge:** "The discovery call is exactly that, twenty minutes, no jargon. If it turns out this isn't a fit, I'll tell you plainly."

## "How much does this cost" / cost skepticism

**Acknowledge:** Reasonable to want to know that early.

**Diagnose:** If the tone suggests sticker shock rather than genuine information-seeking: "What were you expecting, roughly?"

**Reframe:** Answer directly and immediately, no deflection into "it depends" (see `discovery-call-script.md`, the direct-price close). If they push back on the number, connect it to the cost of the problem they described earlier in the call, not to a feature list.

**Bridge:** "Given what you told me it's costing you when [the problem they named] happens, does that number still feel out of range, or is it more that you want to see the scope in writing first?"
