# VSM workshop kit (facilitator guide)

For the Map phase. Run after discovery interviews, before kaizen prioritization. This is your run sheet: what to prep, what to say, what to draw, what usually goes wrong.

Default format is one full day, in person, one process. A half-day compression note is at the end for smaller clients or tighter schedules.

---

## Before the workshop

### Pick the process

Don't ask the client which process to map. Decide from discovery findings and tell them. Good candidates share three traits: it happens often (daily or weekly, not twice a year), it crosses more than one person or role, and someone in discovery already complained about it without you asking.

Examples by industry:
- Home services: quote to invoice, or first call to scheduled job
- Property management: maintenance request to work order closed
- Law firm: intake to signed engagement letter
- Salon: booking to checkout, or new client onboarding

Pick one. Mapping two processes in one day produces two shallow maps instead of one useful one. If the client insists a second process matters more, note it and schedule a second session.

### Who has to be in the room

Rule: everyone who touches the process, not everyone who manages it. A dispatcher who books the job outranks the ops manager who only hears about it in the weekly meeting. If the owner does part of the work themselves (many owners still write quotes or handle first calls), the owner is a participant, not just an observer.

Target 4-6 people. Below 4, you're missing a handoff. Above 8, half the room goes quiet and you're facilitating a meeting instead of running a workshop. If the business is small enough that "everyone who touches it" is 3 people, that's fine. Cap at 8 regardless of company size; split into two sessions if more roles are essential.

Send a short heads-up beforehand: what you're doing, why (so they don't think they're being evaluated), and that there's no wrong answer.

### Materials

Physical (preferred, even for a first-time group):
- Painter's tape, one roll, to lay a horizontal line across a wall or long table (this is the process spine)
- A large roll of paper or several flip chart sheets taped end to end, laid along the tape line
- Sticky notes, at least three colors (one for process steps, one for pain points, one reserved for the AI overlay session later)
- Markers, thick enough that handwriting is readable from six feet away, one per person
- A stopwatch or phone timer, for anyone willing to estimate touch times out loud

Digital alternative (remote or hybrid groups): a Miro or FigJam board, pre-built with a horizontal swimlane template before the session starts. Don't build the template live, it burns 15 minutes of goodwill. Share the link the morning of, not the night before (links found the night before get forgotten).

Either way, bring a camera or make sure someone's phone is charged. The map gets photographed at the end regardless of medium.

### Room and time

Half a wall, minimum, or a table nobody needs during the session. Standing room around three sides of the map. If the room only seats people at a table facing a screen, it is the wrong room, this is a stand-up-and-write workshop.

Block the full day on calendars, including the owner's. The most common failure mode is the owner ducking out for "just this one call."

## Agenda (one day, in-person)

Times assume a 9am start. Shift as needed but keep the proportions.

| Time | Block |
|---|---|
| 9:00 - 9:20 | Welcome, why we map, ground rules |
| 9:20 - 9:50 | SIPOC warm-up |
| 9:50 - 10:00 | Break |
| 10:00 - 12:00 | Current-state mapping walkthrough |
| 12:00 - 12:45 | Lunch |
| 12:45 - 2:15 | Time and wait-time data, pain markers |
| 2:15 - 2:30 | Break |
| 2:30 - 3:15 | Review the full map together, fill gaps |
| 3:15 - 3:45 | Wrap: what we learned, what happens next |
| 3:45 - 4:00 | Photograph/digitize, thank the room |

### 9:00, intro and why we map

Say this or close to it: "Today we're not fixing anything. We're going to draw, on this wall, exactly how a job actually moves through this business right now, not how the training manual says it should. If it takes three tries and two phone calls to get an invoice out, that goes on the wall. Nobody's in trouble for anything we write down today."

Ground rules, said out loud:
- We're mapping the real process, not the ideal one.
- Whoever does the work is the expert on that step, not their manager.
- If something looks dumb on the wall, that's the point. That's what we're here to find.

### 9:20, SIPOC warm-up

Before mapping steps, bound the process so the room agrees on where it starts and ends. Draw a simple table: Supplier, Input, Process, Output, Customer.

Ask the room:
- "What starts this process? What's the trigger?" (a phone call, a form submission, a walk-in)
- "Where does it end, as far as we're concerned today? When is this job done?"
- "Who's the customer of this process?" (usually the external customer, sometimes an internal one, like the field tech being the customer of the dispatcher's step)

This takes 20-30 minutes and prevents the classic failure: an hour into mapping, someone says "well actually it starts before that" and the whole spine shifts.

### 10:00, current-state mapping walkthrough

See the facilitation script below. This is the core of the day.

### 12:45, time and wait-time data, pain markers

Once the skeleton of steps is on the wall, go back through it a second pass, asking for two numbers per step: how long the work itself takes (touch time) and how long it typically sits before anyone picks it up (wait time). Write both on the sticky note under the step. Add a red dot or torn-corner sticky wherever someone in the room winces, hesitates, or says "well, that's supposed to happen but."

### 2:30, review and fill gaps

Walk the full map left to right as a group, out loud, one more time. This is where the quiet people in the room usually correct something. Don't skip this pass to save time, it's where half the real findings surface.

### 3:15, wrap and next step

Say what happens next in plain terms: "I'm going to take this map, photograph it, and clean it up. Next session, we're going to go back through every single step on this wall and mark which ones are good candidates for AI or automation help, which ones need a human no matter what, and why. Then we prioritize what to actually fix first."

## Facilitation script: building the map

Work left to right. One step at a time. Don't let the room jump ahead to step 6 while you're still nailing down step 2, that's how steps get merged incorrectly or skipped.

**Starting point.** "We said the trigger is [the customer calls in with a maintenance problem]. That's step one. Somebody write that on a sticky and put it on the wall."

**The core loop, repeated for every step.** After each sticky goes up, ask:

"Then what happens?"

Wait. Let the room answer, don't answer for them. If two people answer differently, that's data, not a problem to smooth over on the spot: "Hold on, I'm hearing two different answers. Sarah, walk me through what you do. Mike, walk me through what you do." Write both, and mark it as a variation, don't force a single canonical answer.

For each step, capture four things on or near the sticky note:

1. **Actor**: who does this. A role, not a person's name, unless only one person ever does it (write "dispatcher," not "Karen," unless Karen is the only person who will ever hold that role).
2. **System**: what tool or artifact is used. Paper, a phone call, QuickBooks, a shared spreadsheet, a text message, someone's memory.
3. **Touch time**: how long the actual work takes, once someone starts it.
4. **Wait time**: how long it typically sits before the next person touches it.

Keep asking "then what happens?" until you hit the end point you agreed on in the SIPOC warm-up.

**Mark rework loops.** Whenever the group describes a step looping back to an earlier one (a quote gets kicked back for revision, an invoice gets corrected and resent), draw a visible loop-back arrow on the map and mark it. Don't let a rework loop get flattened into a single forward-only step, that loop is often the most expensive part of the process.

**Closing the loop for each step before moving on.** Before advancing, confirm: "So this step is: dispatcher, in the CRM, takes about five minutes, and usually sits for two hours before anyone picks it up. That right?" Getting a nod locks in the step and keeps the room from re-litigating it later.

## Symbol and notation legend (simplified, for sticky notes)

This is not the manufacturing symbol set (no kanban triangles, no supermarket icons, no truck shipment symbols). Office and service processes don't need them. Use this instead:

| Mark | Meaning | How to draw it on a sticky |
|---|---|---|
| Rectangle sticky | A process step | One sticky per step, actor + system + touch time + wait time written on it |
| Arrow between stickies | Flow from one step to the next | Straight line, direction of travel |
| Loop-back arrow | Rework: the step sends work backward to an earlier step | Curved arrow pointing back, labeled with why (e.g. "customer disputes price") |
| Diamond or "?" mark | A decision point where the process forks | Two arrows out, each labeled with the condition |
| Red dot or torn corner | Pain point: the room reacted, hesitated, or flagged it as broken | Placed directly on the step sticky, not a separate note |
| Clock icon or "T=" | Touch time | Written under the step, e.g. "T=5 min" |
| Cloud icon or "W=" | Wait time | Written under the step, e.g. "W=2 hrs" |
| Double vertical bars | A handoff between two different people or systems | Drawn between two stickies where the actor or system changes |

Skip anything from the manufacturing canon that doesn't map to office work: no inventory triangles, no push/pull arrows, no shipment icons. If a symbol needs a footnote to explain what it means in an office context, drop it.

## Facilitation craft

**The boss answers first, every time.** Owners and managers reflexively answer for their staff, out of habit, not malice. When this happens, redirect immediately and by name: "Hang on, let's hear it from whoever actually does this step day to day. [Owner], hold that thought, I want to come back to it." Do this the first time it happens, gently but clearly, or it sets the pattern for the whole day.

**"That's not the official process."** Someone will describe what they actually do, and someone else (usually a manager) will interrupt with "well, that's not how we're supposed to do it." Don't let this correct the map. Say: "Got it, and we'll capture what's supposed to happen too, but right now I want what actually happens on a normal Tuesday. Both are useful, we're starting with the real one."

**Staff afraid to expose workarounds.** If someone built a personal system to cover a gap (a side spreadsheet, a habit of double-checking a coworker's work, a shortcut that isn't documented anywhere), they may hide it out of fear it looks like they're doing something wrong. Normalize it out loud, early: "If you've built your own workaround for something the system doesn't handle, that's not a confession, that's exactly what I need on this wall. It usually means the workaround is doing a job the real process should be doing."

**Mapping the ideal instead of the real.** The room will drift toward describing the process as it's supposed to work, especially with a manager present. Catch drift by asking for a specific recent example: "Walk me through the last actual job like this, not a general description. What happened Tuesday?" Specific instances break the pull toward the idealized version.

**Silence after "then what happens?"** If the room goes quiet, don't fill the silence yourself. Wait it out, or redirect to the person most likely to know: "[Name], you're usually the one who picks this up, what happens next?" Filling silence with your own guess is the fastest way to end up mapping a process that isn't real.

## Common pitfalls

- **Facilitator does too much writing.** Hand the marker to participants when you can. A map the room built with their own hands sticks better than one you drew for them.
- **Merging two variations into one step to save time.** If two people do the step differently, that variation is itself a finding. Don't average it away.
- **Skipping wait time.** Touch time alone hides the real cost. A five-minute step that sits for three days is a bigger problem than a thirty-minute step that starts immediately.
- **Running out of wall before running out of process.** Leave more room than feels necessary before you start. Cramming the end of a map into a corner loses detail exactly where rework loops tend to cluster.
- **Letting one person's phone interrupt three times.** Set the norm at 9am: phones away or on silent, whoever's needed most urgently steps out briefly rather than half-attending.
- **No one photographs the map before it gets taken down or erased.** Assign this task to a specific person by name before lunch, not "someone will grab it later."

## End state

By 4pm, you should have: a full current-state map taped to a wall or built in a digital board, every step tagged with actor, system, touch time, and wait time, rework loops drawn in, pain points marked. Photograph it from a few angles (whole map, then close-ups of any section with dense writing) or, if built digitally, export it as an image and a native file.

This map is the input to the next session: the AI overlay pass, where every step gets annotated for automation potential before the kaizen prioritization workshop. See `vsm-ai-overlay.md`.

## Half-day compression (for smaller clients)

For a business under roughly 15 people, or when a full day isn't available, compress to a half day (about 3.5 hours):

| Time | Block |
|---|---|
| 0:00 - 0:15 | Intro and why we map |
| 0:15 - 0:35 | SIPOC warm-up |
| 0:35 - 2:00 | Current-state mapping (combine the walkthrough and the time/pain pass into one loop, per step: actor, system, touch time, wait time, pain marker, all captured together instead of two separate passes) |
| 2:00 - 2:10 | Break |
| 2:10 - 2:50 | Group review and gap-fill |
| 2:50 - 3:15 | Wrap and next step |
| 3:15 - 3:30 | Photograph and pack up |

What to cut: the separate time-and-pain pass (fold it into the main walkthrough instead of doing two loops through the process), and any temptation to map a second process. What not to cut: the SIPOC warm-up and the final group review pass. Both are short and both are where the room catches its own mistakes.
